01 / THE BASICS

WHAT IS
MORSE CODE?

Morse code is a method of representing letters, numbers, and symbols using short and long signals.

It was developed for early electrical telegraph systems in the 1830s and 1840s and later adapted for radio communication.

THE TWO BUILDING BLOCKS

DOT / DIT

A short signal: 1 time unit.

02 / THE MECHANISM

HOW DOES
IT WORK?

STEP / 01

ENCODE

Turn each letter into its unique pattern of dots and dashes. Example: A becomes ·−.
STEP / 02

TRANSMIT

Send the pattern using sound, a flashing light, electrical pulses, or radio signals.
STEP / 03

DECODE

A receiver recognizes the timing and translates the pattern back into readable text.

Timing matters: 1 unit between parts of a letter, 3 units between letters, and 7 units between words.

04 / WORLD WAR II

THE CODE
IN COMBAT.

When communication was a matter of seconds, Morse offered a practical way to send information across long distances.

A simple signal could travel through radio interference when spoken messages were difficult to understand.

WHY IT MATTERED

Military radio: Operators sent coded traffic using radio transmitters.

Naval operations: Ships and shore stations exchanged messages.

Resistance networks: Some clandestine radio operators transmitted intelligence by Morse.

Important: Morse code is an encoding system, not encryption. Sensitive wartime messages often required separate ciphers.

05 / KEY TAKEAWAY

SMALL SIGNALS.
BIG IMPACT.

Morse code made it possible to communicate with just two signal lengths. From telegraph wires to World War II radio rooms, its strength was simplicity, timing, and reliability.
